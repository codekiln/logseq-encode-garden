Learning/Concept/Homeschooler Mind

Properties
logseq-entity
Logseq/Entity/Concept

  • Homeschooler Mind

    • Homeschooler mind is the habit of taking responsibility for figuring out how to learn something: becoming absorbed in a subject, finding people and examples to study, making a curriculum, and testing what works. The same idea might be called homeschooler brain.
    • A hypothesis: working life may be reaching a point where this kind of self-directed learning becomes necessary to stay afloat. Semiconductor development offers an analogy for why a demanding approach can become worth pursuing after easier approaches have served their purpose.

    • The priority queue of innovations

      • In Dwarkesh Patel’s “What will computer chips look like in 2045?”, the comparison is between FinFET and gate-all-around transistors. The episode dates their laboratory demonstrations to 1989 and 1988, respectively, but their production arrivals to Intel in 2011 and Samsung in 2022. FinFET began reaching volume production around 2011; planar transistors preceded it.
      • Chris Auth explains the sequencing: “Well, you don’t want to tackle something before you have to.” Strained silicon, high-k metal gates, and FinFETs offered improvements before the industry needed to solve gate-all-around’s manufacturing problems. Those problems required materials, tools, and repeated experiments.
      • Related Readwise snips: Why Gate-All-Around Transistors Matter and Semiconductor R&D Became a Golden Age of Innovation.
      • The broader idea is a priority queue of innovations. Knowing that an improvement is possible does not settle when it makes sense to develop it. The sequence depends on what other improvements remain available, the cost of solving each problem, and the payoff within a useful time horizon.
      • Solving a difficult problem too early can consume effort that would produce more value elsewhere. As easier improvements run out, the difficult problem moves up the queue. The financial incentive changes even when the underlying idea has been known for decades.

    • Jerry Seinfeld made his own curriculum

      • In Acquired’s Seinfeld episode, Jerry Seinfeld attends Queens College in New York and sets up an independent study of stand-up comedy. He visits comedy clubs, writes papers analyzing comedians’ sets, and performs himself. He continues studying his own performances and refining his material after college.
      • This is homeschooler mind: an individual obsession becomes a way of learning. The learner decides what to investigate, finds examples in the world, and figures out how to get better.
      • Traditional schooling offers an easier, more legible route: do the assigned work, receive the results, earn the degree. Seinfeld’s approach asks the learner to carve a path and supply the direction. His independent study also shows that this can happen inside a traditional institution.
      • That degree of obsession is hard to prescribe for everyone. It may be neither necessary nor feasible for every person or circumstance.

    • Has self-directed learning moved up the queue?

      • The workforce analogy is about changing incentives. A familiar educational and career path can remain a reasonable choice while it reliably rewards the work put into it. If that path becomes less reliable, the effort of developing homeschooler mind may become more worthwhile, or necessary to remain economically afloat.
      • Perhaps we are reaching the workforce equivalent of the point when semiconductor manufacturers had to tackle gate-all-around. An approach that was once difficult to justify as a general requirement may now address a problem that the easier route no longer solves.
      • This remains a hypothesis about the workforce. The semiconductor story explains how the timing of a difficult investment can change; it leaves open how widely the same pressure applies to workers and how people can develop self-direction when time, support, and appetite for obsession differ.
