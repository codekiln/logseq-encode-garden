  • Aura

    • In Person/Walter Benjamin/Essay/35/The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction, aura is the presence of a unique thing in its own time and place — what a copy of it lacks, and what withers when mechanical reproduction makes copies cheap.

    • Overview

      • Benjamin introduces the word to name what reproduction removes from a work of art:

        • One might subsume the eliminated element in the term "aura" and go on to say: that which withers in the age of mechanical reproduction is the aura of the work of art.
          Reader highlight

    • Key principles

      • Aura depends on an original. Authenticity needs the original's presence, and no technique of reproduction can supply it.

        • The presence of the original is the prerequisite to the concept of authenticity. … The whole sphere of authenticity is outside technical—and, of course, not only technical—reproducibility.
          Reader highlight

      • Aura is a distance. Benjamin illustrates it with natural objects, as something felt across a gap, however near the thing is.

        • We define the aura of the latter as the unique phenomenon of a distance, however close it may be. If, while resting on a summer afternoon, you follow with your eyes a mountain range on the horizon or a branch which casts its shadow over you, you experience the aura of those mountains, of that branch.
          Reader highlight

      • Aura belongs to ritual. It comes from art's cult value, the value of a thing kept for worship. Reproduction shifts the weight to exhibition value, the value of being seen.

        • for the first time in world history, mechanical reproduction emancipates the work of art from its parasitical dependence on ritual.
          Reader highlight

    • Mechanism

      • Reproduction takes a work out of its place and tradition, and puts many copies in place of one existence. Benjamin ties its decay to the masses' wish to bring things closer and to accept a copy in place of the unique thing.

        • the desire of contemporary masses to bring things 'closer' spatially and humanly, which is just as ardent as their bent toward overcoming the uniqueness of every reality by accepting its reproduction.
          Reader highlight

    • Examples

      • Early portrait photographs hold the last of it, in the faces of loved ones.

        • For the last time the aura emanates from the early photographs in the fleeting expression of a human face. This is what constitutes their melancholy, incomparable beauty.
          Reader highlight

      • A photo of the moon. Person/Stephen West describes photographing a beautiful moon and getting a picture of something like a tennis ball: the experience does not survive the copy. Person/Stephen West/Pod/21/03 Episode 152 The Frankfurt School - Walter Benjamin Pt. 1, 15:39 — Moon Photo Anecdote

    • Misconceptions

      • Aura is not the same as quality or beauty. A reproduction can show every detail of a painting and still lack the aura, because the aura is the original's presence in one place and time, not anything visible in the image.
