Book/The Laws of Simplicity/Law 02 - Organize

  • Law 2 — Organize: Organization makes a system of many appear fewer.

    • What it means

      • When you can't reduce the number of things, organize them into groups.
      • Grouping collapses many into few — a wardrobe of a thousand items becomes six categories (neckties, shirts, slacks, jackets, socks, shoes).
      • This works only when the number of groups is significantly smaller than the number of items.

    • Three strategies for managing clutter

      • 1. Buy more space — larger home, bigger drawer, extra storage. Creates room but also invites more stuff.
      • 2. Hide it — put things in storage or out of sight. Effective short-term; clutter returns.
      • 3. Organize systematically — group by principle so things stay findable and manageable.
        • This is the only strategy that scales over time.

    • The key question

      • Most tidying asks "What to hide?" and "Where to put it?"
      • The harder, more powerful question is: "What goes with what?"
        • Good groupings create mental models that make the whole system feel smaller.

    • How to apply it

      • Audit complexity by counting categories, not items. Fewer categories = simpler feel.
      • Design groupings around how users think, not how the system is structured internally.
      • Consistent organizing principles must be easy to follow, or they won't stick.
Book/The Nature and Art of Workmanship

  • The Nature and Art of Workmanship

    • A classic book about how things are made, and how quality emerges from a process—not just from "design" or "materials."

    • Core Concept: Workmanship as Risk

      • Pye defines workmanship as the degree of risk in the making:

      • Workmanship of Certainty

        • The process is set up so the outcome is essentially guaranteed.
        • Examples:
          • Injection molding
          • CNC machining
          • Stamping
          • Mass production jigs/fixtures

      • Workmanship of Risk

        • The quality depends on the maker's real-time judgment and skill—failure is always possible.
        • Examples:
          • Hand carving
          • Turning
          • Hand planing
          • Freehand joinery

    • Why It Matters

      • Pye argues that "craft" is not about romance or tradition—it's about where the risk sits:
        • You can have risk in modern processes (e.g., a skilled operator dialing in a machine)
        • You can have certainty even in "handmade" work if it's heavily constrained by templates/jigs
        • "Mass production" isn't inherently lower quality; it's a different strategy for managing risk

    • The Takeaway Heuristic

      • If you want higher quality, you either:
        • Reduce risk by constraining the process (certainty)
        • Accept risk and pay for skill/time (risk)

    • Connections

      • Related to software engineering concepts:
        • CI/CD = certainty
        • Type systems = certainty
        • Codegen = certainty
        • "Guardrails" = certainty vs "hero coding" = risk

Clade

  • Clade

    • In biology, a clade is a group made up of one common ancestor and all of its descendants. Biologists also call it a monophyletic group.[1]

    • Origin

      • From Ancient Greek kládos, "shoot, branch". The biologist Julian Huxley coined the word in 1957, in the Nature paper "The Three Types of Evolutionary Process".[1] [2]

    • How clades work

      • Clades nest inside one another, because each branch of the tree of life splits into smaller branches.[1]
      • A group that leaves out some descendants of its common ancestor is paraphyletic. The traditional class Reptilia is the usual example: it holds the last common ancestor of reptiles and its descendants, except the birds.[3]
      • Clades are the basic unit of cladistics, the approach to taxonomy that most fields of biology now use.[1]
