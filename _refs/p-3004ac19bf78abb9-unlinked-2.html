AI/Paper/25/07/GEPA Reflective Prompt Evolution Can Outperform Reinforcement Learning

  • [2507.19457] GEPA: Reflective Prompt Evolution Can Outperform Reinforcement Learning

    • Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly adapted to downstream tasks via reinforcement learning (RL) methods like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which often require thousands of rollouts to learn new tasks. We argue that the interpretable nature of language can often provide a much richer learning medium for LLMs, compared with policy gradients derived from sparse, scalar rewards. To test this, we introduce GEPA (Genetic-Pareto), a prompt optimizer that thoroughly incorporates natural language reflection to learn high-level rules from trial and error. Given any AI system containing one or more LLM prompts, GEPA samples system-level trajectories (e.g., reasoning, tool calls, and tool outputs) and reflects on them in natural language to diagnose problems, propose and test prompt updates, and combine complementary lessons from the Pareto frontier of its own attempts. As a result of GEPA's design, it can often turn even just a few rollouts into a large quality gain. Across four tasks, GEPA outperforms GRPO by 10% on average and by up to 20%, while using up to 35x fewer rollouts. GEPA also outperforms the leading prompt optimizer, MIPROv2, by over 10% across two LLMs, and demonstrates promising results as an inference-time search strategy for code optimization.

Archaeology/Tell

  • Tell (archaeology)

    • Overview

      • A tell (from Arabic tall, “mound, small hill”) is an artificial mound: stratified debris from many successive settlements built on the same footprint, plus natural sediment—not a natural hill.
      • Tells are the signature landform of multi-period occupation in archaeology: each layer encodes a phase of building, abandonment, and rebuilding, so the mound is both a landscape feature and a vertical archive of human use.

    • Context

      • Tells are most strongly associated with the ancient Near East (Upper Mesopotamia, Southern Levant, Anatolia, Iran) where continuous settlement in less arid zones allowed debris to outpace erosion.
      • The pattern also appears in Southern and Central Europe (e.g. Greece, Bulgaria, Hungary, Spain), North Africa, and West Africa—anywhere accumulation can win over truncation.
      • In the Southern Levant, the tell-dominated settlement pattern largely ends with the Hellenistic shift after Alexander the Great; elsewhere many tells remain occupied today.
      • English tell entered archaeological usage in the 1840s; field spellings include tel, tall, til, and tal depending on source language and transliteration convention.

    • Key Principles

      • Stratified time depth — Superimposed cultural layers (often labeled by period or phase) make tells prime targets for stratigraphic excavation.
      • Net accumulation — A tell forms only when deposition exceeds removal (erosion, deliberate leveling, modern earthmoving); that constraint explains the tell’s geographic clustering.
      • Terminology is regional — Equivalent mound words include tepe / tappeh (Turkish/Persian), hüyük / höyük (Turkish), kom (Egyptian Arabic), and chogha (Persian); khirbet (“ruin”) often labels ruined tells in toponyms but is not synonymous with tell.
      • Toponymy — Tell is among the most common prefixes in Palestinian place names; the same root appears in biblical Hebrew tel and in Akkadian/Sumerian cognates debated by philologists.

    • Mechanism

      • Mound material mixes organic and cultural refuse, collapsed mudbrick and other building rubble, water-laid and aeolian sediments, and residues of biogenic and geochemical processes on site.
      • The classic profile is a low truncated cone with sloping sides and a flat, mesa-like summit; large examples exceed 40 m in height and can cover tens of hectares at the base.
      • A tell is not born as a mound: the earliest occupation is a flat surface; nucleation of households and repeated rebuilding of daub structures over generations raises the horizon—often over centuries before the form reads clearly as a tell.

    • Examples

      • Near East — Tell es-Sultan (Jericho) among the earliest known examples; Tell Barri in Syria; the Citadel of Aleppo atop a tell occupied since the third millennium BCE; Tel Megiddo in Israel. Surveys have cataloged thousands of tells in the Southern Levant alone.
      • Europe — Neolithic and Chalcolithic tells in northeastern Bulgaria; Greek toumbas (Macedonia) and magoulas (Thessaly), with debate over communal nucleation (Chapman) versus household-scale accumulation (Halstead).
      • Scale — Tell Barri reaches on the order of 30+ m height with a large basal footprint, illustrating how long occupation can dominate local topography.

    • Misconceptions

      • “Every prominent hill in the Levant is a tell” — False; relief must be explained by anthropogenic buildup and stratified settlement, not geology alone.
      • “Tell and tel are interchangeable spellings for one language” — Oversimplified; tel often marks Hebrew toponym transliteration, tell Arabic—same concept, different conventions.
      • “A tell is always abandoned” — False; many tells are still lived on; khirbet (“ruin”) in a name signals destruction, not the tell concept itself.
      • “You can date a tell from its exterior shape alone” — False; chronology comes from excavation, survey, and material culture in strata, not from silhouette.

arXiv/Article/25/11/Word Salad Chopper

  • Word Salad Chopper: Reasoning Models Waste A Ton Of Decoding Budget On Useless Repetitions, Self-Knowingly

    • arXiv:2511.00536 · Wenya Xie, Shaochen (Henry) Zhong, Hoang Anh Duy Le, Zhaozhuo Xu, Jianwen Xie, Zirui Liu

    • Summary

      • Large reasoning models waste significant decoding budget through meaningless token repetitions — and their hidden states reveal the model is self-aware of the loop.
      • A single-layer linear classifier over newline-token hidden states detects word salad during generation with minimal overhead.
      • Removing detected word salad tokens and regenerating yields substantial length savings with minimal quality loss; the authors argue this filtering should be standard practice.

      • "...a significant portion of these tokens are useless self-repetitions—what we call 'word salad'—that exhaust the decoding budget without adding value."

    • Examples in Appendix G

      • Case 1: Semantic Loop from Unresolved Ambiguity (MATH-500).

        • The model begins with valid reasoning but then becomes trapped in a semantic loop — repeating the same confusion without resolution:

        • ‘‘But when I added step-by-step, I got 9997.nn’’
          ‘‘But wait, 6270 + 3737 is 10,007, so why is the step-by-step adding 3000, 700, 30, and 7 giving me 9997?nn’’
          ‘‘But why does the step-by-step addition give me 9997?nn’’ (chopped here)
          ‘‘Wait, so 6270 + 3737 is 10,007...nn’’

        • WSC detects early signs of degeneration and chops at the third chunk, followed by a regeneration prompt. The regenerated continuation quickly resolves the problem with correct reasoning within a 4k budget.

      • Case 2: Endless Enumeration without Convergence (MATH-500).

        • The model attempts a brute-force enumeration without reaching a conclusion:

        • ‘‘For k=1: ...’’
          ‘‘k=12: ...’’
          ‘‘k=14: ...’’ (chopped here)
          ‘‘k=27: ...’’

        • Here, WSC intervenes at chunk 318 to prevent further unbounded enumeration, ensuring the continuation remains within budget. This illustrates WSC’s ability to detect degeneration early and prevent catastrophic repetition.

Audio/Connector/RCA

  • RCA

    • A coaxial connector carrying one signal: a center pin for the signal, an outer shell for ground and shield. Every channel needs its own connector, so a stereo pair takes two and component video takes three.
    • Also called a phono connector, after its first job of wiring a phonograph into a radio receiver, and cinch in France and Germany after the Chicago manufacturer of that name.
    • Introduced by the Radio Corporation of America by 1937 inside the model U-109 radio-phonograph, and on the rear panels of its radios by 1938. Through the 1950s it displaced the 6.35 mm phone connector across consumer hi-fi.
    • Never formally specified. Compatibility rests on manufacturers matching each other, which is why plug impedance varies and composite video over RCA matches 75 Ω only loosely.

      • Audio/Connector/RCA/Stereo — a pair carrying unbalanced analog stereo.

AWS/Whitepaper/Data Classification/Models and Schemes
  • ClassificationDescription
    SensitiveData that is to have the most limited access and requires a high degree of integrity. This is typically data that will do the most damage to the organization should it be disclosed.
    ConfidentialData that might be less restrictive within the company but might cause damage if disclosed.
    PrivatePrivate data is usually compartmental data that might not do the company damage but must be kept private for other reasons. Human resources data is one example of data that can be classified as private.
    ProprietaryProprietary data is data that is disclosed outside the company on a limited basis or contains information that could reduce the company's competitive advantage, such as the technical specifications of a new product.
    PublicPublic data is the least sensitive data used by the company and would cause the least harm if disclosed. This could be anything from data used for marketing to the number of employees in the company.
