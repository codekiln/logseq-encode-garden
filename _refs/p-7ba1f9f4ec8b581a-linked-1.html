Judaism/Concept/Layered Interpretation
Layered Interpretation in Rabbinic JudaismOverview
  • Judaism/Rabbinic is organized around a layered tradition in which later texts interpret earlier texts, preserve the arguments about those interpretations, and then become objects of interpretation themselves. The stack runs from the Judaism/Torah through the Judaism/Mishnah, the Judaism/Gemara and the Judaism/Talmud to medieval commentary and commentary on commentary, with no terminal layer.
Layered Interpretation in Rabbinic JudaismThe stack

  • Judaism/Torah

    • The five books of Moses command without specifying. "Remember the Sabbath day and keep it holy" says nothing about what counts as work, when the day begins, or what may be carried. Interpretation is unavoidable from the first layer.
Layered Interpretation in Rabbinic JudaismThe stack

  • Judaism/Mishnah

    • Compiled around 200 CE under Person/Judah ha-Nasi, the Mishnah reorganizes the law by subject and records disagreement: Rabbi A says X, Rabbi B says Y. It preserves the argument along with the ruling.
Layered Interpretation in Rabbinic JudaismThe stack

  • Judaism/Gemara

    • Centuries of rabbinic discussion of the Mishnah. The Gemara asks why a rabbi holds his view, which verse supports him, whether another Mishnah contradicts him, who the anonymous speaker is, and what unstated assumption would reconcile two statements. Dead ends, objections, hypothetical cases, minority positions and unresolved questions all stay in the record.
Layered Interpretation in Rabbinic JudaismThe stack

  • Judaism/Talmud

    • Mishnah plus Gemara. The Babylonian Talmud, in its received form from the fifth and sixth centuries, became the more influential of the two; the Jerusalem Talmud is a century or so older.
Layered Interpretation in Rabbinic JudaismWritten Torah and Oral Torah
  • Rabbinic Judaism understands revelation as both the Judaism/Torah/Written and the Judaism/Torah/Oral, the interpretive tradition needed to understand and practice it. Interpretation is built into the transmission of revelation, so the chain of interpretation carries religious significance, and disagreement within it counts as part of the tradition rather than as a failure to recover it.
Judaism/Gemara
Gemara
  • The rabbinic discussion of the Judaism/Mishnah, accumulated over centuries and recorded together with the Mishnah as the Judaism/Talmud.
Judaism/Mishnah
Mishnah
  • The early systematic written formulation of the Judaism/Torah/Oral, compiled around 200 CE under Person/Judah ha-Nasi.
Mishnah
  • It organizes Jewish law by subject, such as agriculture, festivals, marriage, civil law, sacrifices and purity, in place of a verse-by-verse commentary, and it records disagreement between named rabbis alongside the rulings. With the Judaism/Gemara it forms the Judaism/Talmud.
Judaism/Rabbinic
Rabbinic Judaism
  • The mainstream form of Judaism since late antiquity, built on the teaching of the rabbis and on the Judaism/Talmud as the authoritative interpretation of the Judaism/Torah.
Rabbinic Judaism
  • It understands revelation as both the Judaism/Torah/Written and the Judaism/Torah/Oral, and it develops as a layered tradition in which each generation's interpretation becomes the next generation's text. Judaism/Concept/Layered Interpretation explains that structure.
Judaism/Talmud
Talmud
  • The Judaism/Mishnah together with the Judaism/Gemara, the central text of Judaism/Rabbinic.
Talmud
  • A traditional printed page places the Mishnah and Gemara at the center and surrounds them with Person/Rashi's commentary, the Tosafists' discussions, cross-references and later notes, so that one page shows generations of readers talking around one text. Judaism/Concept/Layered Interpretation describes the structure the page embodies.
Judaism/Torah
Torah
  • In the narrow sense, the five books of Moses: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy. In the broad sense, Jewish teaching as a whole, so that studying the Judaism/Talmud counts as studying Torah.
Torah
  • The text commands without specifying. "Remember the Sabbath day and keep it holy" leaves open what counts as work, when the day begins and what may be carried, so interpretation starts at the first layer of the tradition. Judaism/Rabbinic distinguishes the Judaism/Torah/Written from the Judaism/Torah/Oral that accompanies it.
Judaism/Torah/Oral
Oral Torah
  • The interpretive tradition that Judaism/Rabbinic holds was revealed alongside the Judaism/Torah/Written and is needed to understand and practice it.
Oral Torah
  • The Judaism/Mishnah wrote down part of it around 200 CE, and the Judaism/Talmud records the arguments over that writing. Because interpretation is built into the transmission of revelation, the chain of interpretation itself carries religious authority.
Judaism/Torah/Written
Written Torah
  • The written Pentateuch, the Judaism/Torah in its narrow sense, as Judaism/Rabbinic distinguishes it from the Judaism/Torah/Oral that is held to have been revealed with it.

Person/Judah ha-Nasi
Judah ha-NasiAbout
  • Rabbi Judah the Prince (about 135 to 217 CE), head of the Sanhedrin in Roman Galilee, under whom the Judaism/Mishnah was compiled around 200 CE.
Person/Maimonides
MaimonidesAbout
  • Moses ben Maimon (1138 to 1204), rabbi, physician and philosopher who lived in Córdoba, Fez and Cairo. His Mishneh Torah restated the whole of Jewish law as a code, leaving out the Judaism/Talmud's arguments, and his Guide for the Perplexed read the tradition through Aristotelian philosophy. Both works became objects of commentary in their turn.
Person/Rashi
RashiAbout
  • Shlomo Yitzchaki (1040 to 1105), rabbi of Troyes in northern France, whose commentaries on the Hebrew Bible and the Judaism/Talmud became the standard companions to both. His Talmud commentary runs down the inner margin of the traditional printed page, and later commentators wrote on his commentary in turn.
