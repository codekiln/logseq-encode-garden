My/Pref/Writing/Avoid Distractors such as Awkward or Superfluous Metaphors
  • By "metaphor" in this page I'm referring to figurative comparison of any kind a true Metaphor, a Simile, an Analogy, or any other comparison.
  • There are more ways to inappropriately use metaphors than to appropriately use them. They are best avoided unless the situation is worth the extra effort. Any metaphor that becomes a Distractor is a smile a toothless mouth. Know what I mean? You don't? Well, there you go, now you understand what it feels like to be distracted by a useless comparison (sorry to do that to you! let's do that less).
  • Keep metaphors special by only using them in places where you need to command the reader's attention in a special way. For example, if there's a crucial aspect of the design of a system that could go very wrong, and you need to talk about trade-offs between alternatives, maybe then it's appropriate to use a metaphor to heighten draw the reader's attention to how special that moment is.

Writing/Q/What's the difference between a metaphor, a simile, and an analogy?
  • Short answer: A Metaphor says one thing is another ("Time is a thief"). A Simile is a metaphor's explicit cousin — it compares using like or as ("Time is like a thief"). An Analogy is bigger than either: it's a structured comparison used to explain or argue, mapping several corresponding parts between two things, often built out of a metaphor or simile.
  • DeviceWhat it assertsMarkerPrimary purpose
    MetaphorOne thing is another (implied identity)none — the equation is implicitVividness, compression, poetry
    SimileOne thing is like anotherthe words "like" or "as"Vivid but explicit comparison
    AnalogyTwo things share a structure of relationshipsoften phrased "A is to B as C is to D"Explanation or argument

  • Metaphor vs. simile

    • The only surface difference is the comparison word. A simile keeps "like" or "as" and states the comparison openly; a metaphor drops them and asserts the identity directly, which reads as stronger and more compressed.
      • Simile: "Her mind is like a steel trap."
      • Metaphor: "Her mind is a steel trap."
    • Many grammarians treat a simile as a subtype of metaphor — both are figures of speech (rhetorical/poetic devices) and both work by likeness; the simile just signals the likeness with an explicit marker.
  • An analogy is less a figure of speech and more a mode of reasoning or explanation. Where a metaphor or simile makes a single vivid likeness, an analogy draws out multiple, systematic correspondences to clarify something unfamiliar or to support an argument.
  • See "like" or "as" → simile. A direct is/are equation → metaphor. An extended comparison doing explanatory or argumentative work → analogy (which may itself contain metaphors and similes).
