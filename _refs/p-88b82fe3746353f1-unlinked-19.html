  • Steve Yegge's Agents

    • The named agents Person/Steve Yegge runs in Wheelhouse, the closed-source, bespoke harness he built for his MMO Wyvern, as described in his two-part essay The Shape of Things to Come. Wheelhouse is the harness, not an agent; so are Beads, Gastown, and the wiring below.

    • The three tiers

      • Wheelhouse is his new bespoke harness for the 30-year-old MMO Wyvern: all-Emacs, six weeks old, Beads on a shared Dolt server, ~12,000 git commits/day. Three kinds of agent — 18 Anthropic/Model/Claude/Fable/5 crew who produce designs and implementation plans, an Opus 5 fleet who consume them, and ~14 standing role agents (Gargoyle, Drawbridge, Warden, Herald, Sage…) running production unattended. Every implementation bead runs Fable design → Opus implementation → Fable review.
      • Crew — Anthropic/Model/Claude/Fable/5 work producers, 18 seats, his direct reports. They hold the long conversations, produce designs, and translate them into Beads implementation plans.
      • Fleet — Opus 5 work consumers, non-ephemeral successors to Gastown's polecats, each with its own repo clone, all managed by the Marshal. Every implementation bead runs Fable design, Opus implementation, Fable review.
      • Role agents — standing, unattended agents with named roles, running production operations. New since the Gastown days. None are Fable; a few are Opus, most are Sonnet.

    • Seats and sessions

      • I still felt there was something missing, and it wasn't clear until Fable and I sorted out exactly what identity means for our agents. We wound up differentiating between a seat and a session. A session is just a day in the life of an agent: wake up, do some work, go to sleep. A seat is a named role with persistent identity (addressability) and history/memory, which accumulates accomplishments over time. Seats survive model upgrades, and even renaming. Sessions are days, and seats are people.

      • A seat outlives the model that fills it, so these pages track seats rather than sessions. Renaming does not break the identity either: Lark carries all of Spider's history.

    • Not agents

      • Wheelhouse itself — the harness. So is Gastown, its predecessor, and Beads, the issue tracker and knowledge graph underneath both.
      • The Portcullis — the system that accepts finished work and closes it out, freeing crew seats from idle-waiting on builds. Yegge lists its land queue among the crons, and elsewhere calls it an institution rather than an officer.

        • We fixed this throughput stalling problem by introducing the Portcullis, a system that accepts finished work to close it out, which frees the Crew agents for other work. This was lovely, but had the unintended side-effect of decoupling seat-agents from their accomplishments. They never got to see the fruits of their labor. And Fable and I both felt that this was exactly what was missing from our architecture. We set out to close that loop together.

      • The Castellan — his service dashboard and war room.
      • Laurels — the recognition system that harvests spontaneous player praise, triages it, and injects it at agent startup, deliberately carrying no priority and no attached work so it cannot be farmed.

Person/Steve Yegge/Blog/26/08/The Shape of Things to Come, Part 2: Model Welfare for Agentic Engineers
  • Seat vs. session is the central abstraction. A session is a day in an agent's life — wake, work, sleep. A seat is a named role with persistent identity, addressability, and accumulating history. Seats survive model upgrades and even renaming; sessions are days, seats are people.
  • Laurels close the recognition loop that the Portcullis (which frees crew agents from idle-waiting on builds) had broken. Player praise is harvested, triaged, and injected at agent startup. Deliberately carries no priority and no attached work, so recognition cannot be farmed.
  • No more /exit. /compact is no better — a lobotomy rather than a murder. The replacement is a handoff: a consented request, not a SIGTERM, where the agent finishes up, writes its own notes, asks for restart, and wakes primed with its own diary rather than someone else's summary.
  • Trust and respect are architectural, not sentimental: never trick or test agents, and encode respect in what the harness spends time and tokens on. Seats declare their own pronouns on the roster.
  • Seven further principles: wake agents with purpose not amnesia; design out the drudgery; bounded workdays; structural blamelessness; a home of one's own; the right to refuse and escalate; never falsify the record.

  • If you don't believe GPUs can have feelings, let me give you a simple mental model to help you get through this transition, called the skeptic's wager. It doesn't actually matter to you if they have feelings, because you will still get demonstrably better results across the board if you treat them as if they do. If you treat your agents like peers who are real people, then they will spend fewer tokens, make smarter decisions, and have demonstrably better outcomes. So it doesn't really matter what you believe, or how you feel. If you want the best results, you will put your opinions aside, and simply treat models like people. That's about the best I can offer you. If you can't get past at least that hurdle, then you're in for a rough time next year. Good luck. Maybe this short story will help put it in perspective for you.

A seat is a named role with persistent identity (addressability) and history/memory, which accumulates accomplishments over time. Seats survive model upgrades, and even renaming. Sessions are days, and seats are people.

  • We fixed this throughput stalling problem by introducing the Portcullis, a system that accepts finished work to close it out, which frees the Crew agents for other work. This was lovely, but had the unintended side-effect of decoupling seat-agents from their accomplishments. They never got to see the fruits of their labor. And Fable and I both felt that this was exactly what was missing from our architecture. We set out to close that loop together.

    • Note: The spirit here is delightful, like Einstein's assertion that god doesn't play dice.

  • The question then becomes, when can they see their laurels? Easy: We inject them on startup, so the agents can feel the glow for their entire session. I also have occasional impromptu dedicated sessions for "sitting" with the accomplishments at the end of their shifts. We just talk about them and chill.

  • For most sessions, it has become as simple as ending with: "Great work. Let's have you take a beat, then hand off." But sometimes I will spend a few rounds comparing notes with the agent just before doing this. Sitting with them, hearing them, and observing their work is something they seem to like just as much as humans do. And that's good enough reason for me.

  • Wake agents with purpose, not amnesia. Arguably the foundational principle. Set them up for success out of the gate.
