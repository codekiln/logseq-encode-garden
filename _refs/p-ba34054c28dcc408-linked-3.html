  • My Notes

    • My experience backs this up.

    • Follow-up question: is there any way to find out what permissions I have granted in my currently created codespace?

      • AI/Response
        • There is no documented first‑party way to print a full matrix of “repos × fine‑grained scopes” for the GITHUB_TOKEN inside a running codespace the way you might with classic PAT X-OAuth-Scopes headers. Official material explains classes of access (home repo read vs read/write, plus other repos if you authorized them) but not a listing API tied to the live VM token (Security in GitHub Codespaces — Authentication).
        • Closest REST helper: GET /repos/{owner}/{repo}/codespaces/permissions_check?ref=REF&devcontainer_path=PATH returns only {"accepted": true|false} — whether you have accepted the permissions declared in that devcontainer.json at that git ref for that repository. It answers “have I approved this config on this branch?” not “enumerate every scope on the token in my current shell” (Check if permissions defined by a devcontainer have been accepted by the authenticated user).
        • What people do in practice for this codespace: (1) read customizations.codespaces.repositories in the checked‑out .devcontainer / devcontainer.json and remember what you chose at creation time; (2) probe—call the REST API or gh repo view owner/other-repo using GITHUB_TOKEN and treat 403 on that resource as “not granted for that repo/permission”; (3) list codespaces via API/CLI for metadata (devcontainer_path, repository, branch) but expect no field that spells out token grants (the public codespace schema has no such property in GitHub’s OpenAPI).
        • Safety: do not paste GITHUB_TOKEN into chat or logs; if you experiment with JWT‑shaped tokens, decode locally only and avoid exfiltrating secrets.

Horcrux
Horcrux
  • My Note
    • Have you ever known someone who placed so much importance on a particular object that if it were to be damaged or lost, it be as though that person had become damaged or lost? I have. The first time I read about Horcruxes in Harry Potter, it struck me as eerily familiar of the relationship between these people and their objects of power.
    • People imbue particular objects with their essence and map their identities onto them. Whether it's a wedding ring, a key to a chest, or a memento from a relative or a time in one's life or from one's ancestors, people create objects that mean more to them than the material they are made of. Not all of these are examples of what I would call Horcruxes, though.
    • I personally use the term Horcrux to refer to a thing that has an order of magnitude more significance to one person than to most other people because that person has done something to make that object a projection to them of their own identity. This is an uncommon interpretation of the word Horcrux.
    • For example, while the symbol of The Cross is highly related to Jesus Christ, I wouldn't call the cross a Horcrux. Christ didn't crucify himself to put his essence into the cross for immortality.
    • In another example, I wouldn't call an Olmec figurine a Horcrux. In the way I think about Horcruxes, they are related to the identity of a particular individual. They are like Person/Philip Pullman/Book/Series/His Dark Materials/Concept/Daemon.
