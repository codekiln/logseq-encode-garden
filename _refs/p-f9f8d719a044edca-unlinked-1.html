  • The Art of Raising a Puppy

    • About

      • Written by the Monks of New Skete, the monastic community in Cambridge, New York that breeds and trains German shepherds and supports itself partly by that work.
      • Covers the decision to take a puppy on, choosing a breed, preparing a house, the stages of a puppy's development, how to communicate with it, basic obedience, and common trouble such as chewing, jumping up and paper-training.
      • Follows the community's earlier book How to Be Your Dog's Best Friend. First published in 1991 and revised since.

    • A reading that may carry over to agent work

      • The book is about dogs. What follows is a tentative reading of it, not a claim the authors make.
      • Raising a puppy is presented as development over time rather than a set of commands issued once. Which stage the animal is at decides what it can learn; a correction means nothing unless it arrives at the moment of the act; consistency of handling counts for more than any single technique. The book is also direct about the handler's own behaviour being part of what forms the animal.
      • Those are the parts that resemble instructing an AI agent under My/AI/Rule: what the thing can take in at this point, when feedback has to land to be understood, and how much of the result comes from the instructor being consistent.

Book/The Nature and Art of Workmanship

Page reference

  • The Nature and Art of Workmanship

    • A classic book about how things are made, and how quality emerges from a process—not just from "design" or "materials."

    • Core Concept: Workmanship as Risk

      • Pye defines workmanship as the degree of risk in the making:

      • Workmanship of Certainty

        • The process is set up so the outcome is essentially guaranteed.
        • Examples:
          • Injection molding
          • CNC machining
          • Stamping
          • Mass production jigs/fixtures

      • Workmanship of Risk

        • The quality depends on the maker's real-time judgment and skill—failure is always possible.
        • Examples:
          • Hand carving
          • Turning
          • Hand planing
          • Freehand joinery

    • Why It Matters

      • Pye argues that "craft" is not about romance or tradition—it's about where the risk sits:
        • You can have risk in modern processes (e.g., a skilled operator dialing in a machine)
        • You can have certainty even in "handmade" work if it's heavily constrained by templates/jigs
        • "Mass production" isn't inherently lower quality; it's a different strategy for managing risk

    • The Takeaway Heuristic

      • If you want higher quality, you either:
        • Reduce risk by constraining the process (certainty)
        • Accept risk and pay for skill/time (risk)

    • Connections

      • Related to software engineering concepts:
        • CI/CD = certainty
        • Type systems = certainty
        • Codegen = certainty
        • "Guardrails" = certainty vs "hero coding" = risk

ICMC/25/d1/1400 Concert n02/08 Ars Suita
  • Ars Suita (the art of the suite) is an acousmatic work that revisits the panegyric trio suite “Concert instrumental sous le titre d`Apothéose de Monsieur de Lully”, composed by Person/Francois Couperin (1668-1733) and published in Paris in 1735. This suite serves as an homage to the French composer Person/Jean-Baptiste Lully, who was one of the most important composers of the French Baroque era. As the music director of the royal court of France, Lully’s compositions had a significant impact on French musical style. Couperin’s “Apotheosis” is a suite of pieces that includes a prelude, several dances, and a final chaconne. The suite showcases Couperin’s skill in composing for a variety of instrumental combinations, including strings, winds, and continuo. Its graceful melodies, refined harmonies, and intricate counterpoint are all characteristic of the French Baroque style.
    • Ars Suita is structured in nine movements that follow the same structure as the original piece by Couperin. The material for each of the movements is derived from an audio recording of the original “Apotheosis” with early music instruments. Each movement uses a different sound processing technique, creating a particular sonic character for each part of this acousmatic suite.
    • Like many Baroque composers, Couperin paid tribute to his predecessors and contemporaries through his music. In the same spirit, Ars Suita is a small homage to Couperin, and indirectly to Lully. Composed in a home studio and mastered at CITALAB, Department of Electrical Engineering, Campus San Joaquín, Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile.
