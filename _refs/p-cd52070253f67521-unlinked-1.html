Ad/Block/er/uBlock/Concept/uBlock vs Origin vs Lite
  • Custom Filters and Dynamic Filtering: uBOL does not support custom user-defined filters or dynamic filtering of scripts and iframes. This means users cannot create or modify filter lists beyond what is provided by default. Wikipedia
  • Per-Site Settings: The ability to configure per-site blocking rules or exceptions is absent in uBOL. Users cannot tailor the extension's behavior for specific websites. Wikipedia
  • Element Picker and Cosmetic Filtering: uBOL lacks tools for hiding specific elements on a webpage or applying cosmetic filters to adjust the appearance of a site. Wikipedia
  • Advanced Logging and Debugging Tools: The advanced logger and debugging tools available in uBlock Origin are not present in uBOL, limiting users' ability to diagnose and troubleshoot filtering issues. Wikipedia
  • Advanced Privacy Features: uBOL offers limited privacy protections compared to uBlock Origin. For instance, it does not support advanced features like CNAME uncloaking, which helps in identifying and blocking trackers that use deceptive domain names. Wikipedia
  • Filter List Updates: In uBOL, filter lists are updated only when the extension itself is updated, rather than receiving real-time updates as in uBlock Origin. This can result in slower response to emerging threats. Wikipedia

AI/Concept/Explore/vs/Exploit
  • The formal problem is the multi-armed bandit: a row of slot machines with unknown payout rates and a limited number of pulls to spend on them. Allied statisticians worked on it during the Second World War and found it so hard that Peter Whittle joked it should be dropped on Germany to waste enemy scientists' time. Herbert Robbins gave the problem its standard form in 1952 in "Some aspects of the sequential design of experiments".
  • In AI/Reinforcement Learning the balance is written into the algorithms (Exploration–exploitation dilemma, Wikipedia). Epsilon-greedy takes the best-known action most of the time and a random one otherwise. Upper-confidence-bound methods add an optimism bonus to actions tried less often. Thompson sampling draws a guess at each payoff from what has been observed and acts as if the draw were true. Exploration bonuses turn curiosity into a reward of its own, so the agent can exploit it.

Archaeology/Tell

  • Tell (archaeology)

    • Overview

      • A tell (from Arabic tall, “mound, small hill”) is an artificial mound: stratified debris from many successive settlements built on the same footprint, plus natural sediment—not a natural hill.
      • Tells are the signature landform of multi-period occupation in archaeology: each layer encodes a phase of building, abandonment, and rebuilding, so the mound is both a landscape feature and a vertical archive of human use.

    • Context

      • Tells are most strongly associated with the ancient Near East (Upper Mesopotamia, Southern Levant, Anatolia, Iran) where continuous settlement in less arid zones allowed debris to outpace erosion.
      • The pattern also appears in Southern and Central Europe (e.g. Greece, Bulgaria, Hungary, Spain), North Africa, and West Africa—anywhere accumulation can win over truncation.
      • In the Southern Levant, the tell-dominated settlement pattern largely ends with the Hellenistic shift after Alexander the Great; elsewhere many tells remain occupied today.
      • English tell entered archaeological usage in the 1840s; field spellings include tel, tall, til, and tal depending on source language and transliteration convention.

    • Key Principles

      • Stratified time depth — Superimposed cultural layers (often labeled by period or phase) make tells prime targets for stratigraphic excavation.
      • Net accumulation — A tell forms only when deposition exceeds removal (erosion, deliberate leveling, modern earthmoving); that constraint explains the tell’s geographic clustering.
      • Terminology is regional — Equivalent mound words include tepe / tappeh (Turkish/Persian), hüyük / höyük (Turkish), kom (Egyptian Arabic), and chogha (Persian); khirbet (“ruin”) often labels ruined tells in toponyms but is not synonymous with tell.
      • Toponymy — Tell is among the most common prefixes in Palestinian place names; the same root appears in biblical Hebrew tel and in Akkadian/Sumerian cognates debated by philologists.

    • Mechanism

      • Mound material mixes organic and cultural refuse, collapsed mudbrick and other building rubble, water-laid and aeolian sediments, and residues of biogenic and geochemical processes on site.
      • The classic profile is a low truncated cone with sloping sides and a flat, mesa-like summit; large examples exceed 40 m in height and can cover tens of hectares at the base.
      • A tell is not born as a mound: the earliest occupation is a flat surface; nucleation of households and repeated rebuilding of daub structures over generations raises the horizon—often over centuries before the form reads clearly as a tell.

    • Examples

      • Near East — Tell es-Sultan (Jericho) among the earliest known examples; Tell Barri in Syria; the Citadel of Aleppo atop a tell occupied since the third millennium BCE; Tel Megiddo in Israel. Surveys have cataloged thousands of tells in the Southern Levant alone.
      • Europe — Neolithic and Chalcolithic tells in northeastern Bulgaria; Greek toumbas (Macedonia) and magoulas (Thessaly), with debate over communal nucleation (Chapman) versus household-scale accumulation (Halstead).
      • Scale — Tell Barri reaches on the order of 30+ m height with a large basal footprint, illustrating how long occupation can dominate local topography.

    • Misconceptions

      • “Every prominent hill in the Levant is a tell” — False; relief must be explained by anthropogenic buildup and stratified settlement, not geology alone.
      • “Tell and tel are interchangeable spellings for one language” — Oversimplified; tel often marks Hebrew toponym transliteration, tell Arabic—same concept, different conventions.
      • “A tell is always abandoned” — False; many tells are still lived on; khirbet (“ruin”) in a name signals destruction, not the tell concept itself.
      • “You can date a tell from its exterior shape alone” — False; chronology comes from excavation, survey, and material culture in strata, not from silhouette.

Atlassian/Confluence/API/Concept/GraphQL vs REST
  • Write Operations: Both APIs support CRUD operations, but with different approaches. GraphQL provides mutations like createPage, updatePage, and deletePage for content management. REST provides comprehensive endpoints including dedicated operations like copying pages (POST /wiki/rest/api/content/{id}/copy) that don't have direct GraphQL equivalents
  • Endpoints are organized by resource type (e.g., /wiki/rest/api/content, /wiki/rest/api/space)
  • Request 1: GET /wiki/rest/api/content/{id} to get page details
  • Request 2: GET /wiki/rest/api/space/{spaceKey} to get space information
  • Duplicating Pages: While both APIs can duplicate pages, REST provides a dedicated copy endpoint (POST /wiki/rest/api/content/{id}/copy), while GraphQL requires fetching the original page content via query, then creating a new page via mutation

Audio/Connector/Phone

  • Phone Connector (audio)

    • These have jacks and plugs.

    • Sample Visual Reference

      • Image: CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons

    • Overview

      • Quick reference guide to the 3.5 mm (1/8 in) mini phone connector family, sometimes called the "mini-jack" or "mini audio jack"
      • A miniaturized version of the original 1/4 in (6.35 mm) phone connector
      • Same physical plug size across variants; the number of conductor bands (tip/ring/sleeve) determines what the connector can carry

    • Connector Comparison

      • ConnectorConductorsTypical Use
        Audio/Connector/Phone/TS/3.5mm2 (tip, sleeve)Mono unbalanced audio
        Audio/Connector/Phone/TRS/3.5mm3 (tip, ring, sleeve)Stereo unbalanced audio
        Audio/Connector/Phone/TRRS/3.5mm4 (tip, ring, ring, sleeve)Stereo audio + microphone (headset)

    • Related Sizes (not covered in detail here)

      • 2.5 mm (sub-mini): same tip/ring/sleeve concept, used on some older cordless phones and two-way radios
      • 6.35 mm / 1/4 in (standard phone connector): the original size, common in professional audio (guitar cables, studio patch bays)

    • TRRS Wiring Standards

      • TRRS adds a mic/ground ring, but two incompatible wiring orders exist:
        • CTIA/AHJ (Apple, most modern Android/Windows devices): tip = left, ring 1 = right, ring 2 = ground, sleeve = mic
        • OMTP (older Nokia and some Asian-market phones): tip = left, ring 1 = right, ring 2 = mic, sleeve = ground
      • Plugging a headset wired to one standard into a jack wired to the other leaves left/right audio unaffected but the microphone silent, since only the mic/ground pair is swapped

Audio/Connector/RCA

  • RCA

    • A coaxial connector carrying one signal: a center pin for the signal, an outer shell for ground and shield. Every channel needs its own connector, so a stereo pair takes two and component video takes three.
    • Also called a phono connector, after its first job of wiring a phonograph into a radio receiver, and cinch in France and Germany after the Chicago manufacturer of that name.
    • Introduced by the Radio Corporation of America by 1937 inside the model U-109 radio-phonograph, and on the rear panels of its radios by 1938. Through the 1950s it displaced the 6.35 mm phone connector across consumer hi-fi.
    • Never formally specified. Compatibility rests on manufacturers matching each other, which is why plug impedance varies and composite video over RCA matches 75 Ω only loosely.

    • Wirings

      • Audio/Connector/RCA/Stereo — a pair carrying unbalanced analog stereo.

      • RCA/Connector/Ref/Index — color coding across audio and video, and the plug and jack pages.

Clade

  • Clade

    • In biology, a clade is a group made up of one common ancestor and all of its descendants. Biologists also call it a monophyletic group.[1]

    • Origin

      • From Ancient Greek kládos, "shoot, branch". The biologist Julian Huxley coined the word in 1957, in the Nature paper "The Three Types of Evolutionary Process".[1] [2]

    • How clades work

      • Clades nest inside one another, because each branch of the tree of life splits into smaller branches.[1]
      • A group that leaves out some descendants of its common ancestor is paraphyletic. The traditional class Reptilia is the usual example: it holds the last common ancestor of reptiles and its descendants, except the birds.[3]
      • Clades are the basic unit of cladistics, the approach to taxonomy that most fields of biology now use.[1]
