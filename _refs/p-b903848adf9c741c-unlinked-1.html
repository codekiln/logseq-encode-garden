Book/Clear and Simple as the Truth
  • Subtitled Writing Classic Prose. Published by Princeton University Press in 1994; the Princeton Legacy Library reissued it in 2017.
  • The book treats style as a set of decisions about the writer's relationship to the subject and to the reader, rather than as polish applied to sentences that are already written. Its argument against rule lists is that attending to grammar, sense and sentence structure produces effective prose no more reliably than knowing the mechanics of a golf swing produces a hole-in-one.
  • Classic style rests on four things the writer assumes: that the motive is truth, that the purpose is presentation, that the reader is an intellectual equal, and that the occasion is informal. Prose written on those assumptions reads as a window onto something the writer has seen, and the writer does the work so that the reader does not have to.
  • One place the book and the garden part company: Thomas and Turner hold plain style apart from classic style, because plain style assumes a truth held in common and distrusts individual distinction, while classic style presumes a writer who has seen something personally. My/Pref/Writing/Use Plain language tables readability targets, and nothing in the book's account of classic style supports measuring prose that way.
Book/The Elements of Style
  • Book/Clear and Simple as the Truth is written against books of this kind. Thomas and Turner argue that rules of grammar, sense and sentence structure do not by themselves produce effective prose, and that step-by-step programmes make the problem worse by ignoring that there are several styles with different standards. What they offer instead is a stance the writer takes toward truth, reader and occasion.

Writing/Q/What's the difference between terms like prose, poetry, prosody, poesy, and related terms?

  • What's the difference between terms like prose, poetry, prosody, poesy, and related terms?

    • AI Answer

      • Short answer: the real formal split is Prose vs. Verse, not prose vs. poetry. Poetry is the broader art form that's usually written in verse but doesn't have to be. Prosody studies the sound patterns underneath verse and speech. Poesy is just an archaic word for poetry.

      • Prose vs. Verse — the real formal split

        • The most basic split in written language isn't "prose vs. poetry" — it's prose vs. verse. Prose runs to the margin and wraps naturally; verse is deliberately broken into lines, usually to a metrical or rhythmic pattern.

      • Poetry vs. Verse

        • Verse describes the form: metrical, lineated language. Poetry describes the effect: heightened, musical, imagistic language used to convey feeling or meaning. Most poetry is written in verse, but prose poetry shows the two can separate — it keeps poetry's devices (compression, imagery, rhythm, figurative language) while running as unbroken prose.

      • Prosody

        • Prosody doesn't name a genre or form at all — it names the sound system underneath verse (and, in linguistics, underneath speech generally): meter, stress, rhythm, pitch, intonation. Scanning a poem's meter is doing prosody.

      • Poesy

        • Poesy is an older word (Middle English, via French, from Greek poiesis, "making") that simply means poetry or the art of poetry. It survives mainly in older or self-consciously archaic writing (e.g., Sir Philip Sidney's The Defence of Poesy) and carries no distinct modern meaning beyond that — treat it as a period synonym for poetry.

      • Note

        • Not to be confused with the PyPoetry page in this garden, which covers the Python package manager — unrelated to the literary genre.
