  • The art of Design is a creative seance that begins with a prelude of Questions. Its acolytes construct the liturgy of creation with a prelude of questions to consider before taking any action, making any decision or making anything at all. Each question is a Sample, a marble pulled from a bag, each with a different quality, valence and relevance, that we put up to our eye and that we look through before proceeding, begging us to "choose wisely."
    • What perspectives should we consider?
      • What relevant personas do we not understand enough yet, and what can we do to listen deeper to the people that have them?
    • What would be ideal?
      • In an ideal world with no constraints, what would be possible?
    • What problems exist?
      • Which problems have been hidden, overlooked?
    • What is our purpose?
      • What is our mission?
    • Which creative constraints might be practical or fruitful here?
    • What hypotheses do we have?
      • What tests could we run?
    • What precedents exist?
  • Physicists first articulated the Physics/Observer/Effect after bumping up against the impossibility of perfect objectivity. The way we observe impacts the things we observe. Design flips the script here, in a gesture of Paradox/ical Hubris, asserting that the way we ask questions impacts the things we later make.
  • Lately I can't seem to shake the feeling that the art of design is being challenged by the emergence of AI.
    • We can build something quickly, and then that thing may, or may not, or may to a certain degree result in killing our want for it.
      • Like many people, I'm pro-prototype. I love the idea of them, I love doing them, I love exploring the possibility space.
    • But, design is turning into photography. Photography is a different sort of art. It's about being in many different sorts of places, meeting different sorts of people, taking many shots, and removing many shots. It's very different than painting the Mona Lisa. It's got a bit more of the aspect of a Slot/Machine; it's subject to a Poisson process: yes, no, no, no, yes, no, no.
  • What chance does design have of surviving contact with AI? It's so much more exciting to do than to consider. As AI emerges, will a prelude of questions still be deemed to a necessary antecedent to the act of creation? Humans Satis/fice. Where will we get our recommended daily intake of Vitamin Struggle?

  • The concept of context rot has really come to shape my thoughts about AI assisted dev recently. It's given me a word to apply to that all-too-familiar feeling of when, after the LLM has started to work on a coding task for a while, ingesting the context of the codebase and the back-and forth, it stops adhering to prompting guidelines (CursorAI/.cursor/rules, Claude/Code/Claude.md files, etc). As a result, it's really important to have an alert respect for the context window of various models, and know where you're at relative to those context windows.Model providers use benchmarks like needle in a haystack to make it seem like the entire 200k or 500k context window is equally effective, but as this Chroma paper shows, what actually happens is that as the context window fills up, the ability of the model looses its ability to do complex reasoning, and stops adhering to instructions.My current mental model is that at the first token, the models have 100% of their "brain" available to predict the next token. As you add tokens, it gradually fills up the "short term memory" in such a way that decreases the amount of "attention" it can spend on a task. By the time you get to 50% of the context window, the amount of "space" the LLM has to reason is often too small for the level of accuracy needed in coding.This is why it's not currently a viable technique to just say "go do this" when working on AI coding. Instead, one should break the task into a plan and then into steps that can each be achieved with a certain margin of error, what I'll call the "context buffer," the unused context which is like "space" that allows the model to actually "think." Ableton/Live/Report/26/09/Open-source options for controlling Live 11 from coding agents
  • The creator, Wouter Stierhout, has a comparatively sparse GitHub footprint; public professional search results associate him with Hypersolid, but I found no evidence that Hypersolid publishes, supports, or endorses this project. [24] I therefore regard the project as an individual developer's new open-source project, not a company-supported product. The abletonmcp.com marketing page is more confident than the project’s age warrants; the repository is a stronger trust signal. The phrase about there being no repository to clone appears to be trying to say that users can install the PyPI package with uvx, not that no source repository exists. The underlying design really is local: stdio MCP → loopback TCP → Live Remote Script. [25]
  • uisato/ableton-mcp-extended explicitly advertises Live 11+ and adds significantly more functionality around the same general Remote Script/socket design, including optional higher-performance UDP communication. The inspected project had nontrivial adoption, but its creator's public GitHub footprint is essentially centered on this project and I found no established-company affiliation comparable to the stronger examples above. [9] Its optional ElevenLabs integration is outside the “free-only” criterion when it requires a paid external service, so I would evaluate only the core Ableton integration. More network transports and more tools are not inherently insecure, but they enlarge the amount of code and protocol surface one has to audit. Given that ahujasid and Wouter already cover this architecture with stronger security signals, I see little security-driven reason to prefer the extended version.
  • Finally, Ben Schoepke's ableton-live-mcp is worth knowing about precisely because its design illustrates what I would not expose to an autonomous coding agent on an important machine. The project's README explicitly says the agent can evaluate arbitrary Python running inside Ableton, while also providing optimized tools for common operations. [11] That gives it extraordinary flexibility but largely destroys capability isolation: instead of “the agent may create a clip and set a parameter,” the primitive is effectively “the agent may execute Python in the DAW's embedded interpreter.” The author also warns users to back up sets, and the version I examined documented testing on Live 12 rather than Live 11, so I do not count it as a verified Live-11 option. [11]
  • For an AI agent, I would prioritize capability-limited tools over a generic interpreter even though Codex or Claude Code can themselves execute shell commands. These are different trust boundaries. A coding agent's host execution can be controlled with sandboxing and approval policies; an unrestricted eval endpoint inside Ableton places the Live process, Live's embedded runtime, and whatever that runtime can reach directly behind a model-selected tool call. Schoepke's explicit arbitrary-Python design therefore carries qualitatively more risk than the typed-tool approach used by Wouter and ahujasid. [11]
  • Best option for building your own minimal interface: AbletonOSC/PyLive, after fixing network exposure. For an AI architect, this may actually be the most appealing long-term design. AbletonOSC is older, smaller conceptually, Live-11-native, well understood, and already has both a programmatic OSC API and interactive console. [7] Put it on loopback instead of its default 0.0.0.0, expose only a dozen commands through a tiny local stdio MCP server or CLI, and you have a much smaller reviewable trusted-computing base than a 300-tool agent framework. The stock wildcard bind is the part I would not accept. [33]
  • The broader conclusion is encouraging: Live 11 is not stranded for agentic control, but the strongest choices are community bridges rather than supported external automation APIs. If minimizing dependence on Ableton's private Python internals matters most, Producer Pal is in a class by itself because it builds on the documented Max for Live API. If minimizing network and arbitrary-code exposure matters most, Wouter's typed loopback MCP currently has the cleanest default design, albeit with very little age behind it. If longevity and auditability matter most, AbletonOSC/PyLive plus a small custom, loopback-only agent adapter is arguably the most conservative engineering foundation—provided you correct AbletonOSC's unsafe wildcard listener first. [1]
