Layered Interpretation in Rabbinic JudaismOverview
  • Judaism/Rabbinic is organized around a layered tradition in which later texts interpret earlier texts, preserve the arguments about those interpretations, and then become objects of interpretation themselves. The stack runs from the Judaism/Torah through the Judaism/Mishnah, the Judaism/Gemara and the Judaism/Talmud to medieval commentary and commentary on commentary, with no terminal layer.
Layered Interpretation in Rabbinic JudaismThe stack

  • Judaism/Talmud

    • Mishnah plus Gemara. The Babylonian Talmud, in its received form from the fifth and sixth centuries, became the more influential of the two; the Jerusalem Talmud is a century or so older.
Judaism/Gemara
Gemara
  • The rabbinic discussion of the Judaism/Mishnah, accumulated over centuries and recorded together with the Mishnah as the Judaism/Talmud.
Judaism/Mishnah
Mishnah
  • It organizes Jewish law by subject, such as agriculture, festivals, marriage, civil law, sacrifices and purity, in place of a verse-by-verse commentary, and it records disagreement between named rabbis alongside the rulings. With the Judaism/Gemara it forms the Judaism/Talmud.
Judaism/Rabbinic
Rabbinic Judaism
  • The mainstream form of Judaism since late antiquity, built on the teaching of the rabbis and on the Judaism/Talmud as the authoritative interpretation of the Judaism/Torah.
Judaism/Torah
Torah
  • In the narrow sense, the five books of Moses: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy. In the broad sense, Jewish teaching as a whole, so that studying the Judaism/Talmud counts as studying Torah.
Judaism/Torah/Oral
Oral Torah
  • The Judaism/Mishnah wrote down part of it around 200 CE, and the Judaism/Talmud records the arguments over that writing. Because interpretation is built into the transmission of revelation, the chain of interpretation itself carries religious authority.
Person/Maimonides
MaimonidesAbout
  • Moses ben Maimon (1138 to 1204), rabbi, physician and philosopher who lived in Córdoba, Fez and Cairo. His Mishneh Torah restated the whole of Jewish law as a code, leaving out the Judaism/Talmud's arguments, and his Guide for the Perplexed read the tradition through Aristotelian philosophy. Both works became objects of commentary in their turn.
Person/Rashi
RashiAbout
  • Shlomo Yitzchaki (1040 to 1105), rabbi of Troyes in northern France, whose commentaries on the Hebrew Bible and the Judaism/Talmud became the standard companions to both. His Talmud commentary runs down the inner margin of the traditional printed page, and later commentators wrote on his commentary in turn.
