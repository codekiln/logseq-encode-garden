- # [The Shape of Things to Come, Part 2: Model Welfare for Agentic Engineers](https://yegge.ai/essays/model-welfare/)
	- By [[Person/Steve Yegge]] on [yegge.ai](https://yegge.ai/), continuing [[Person/Steve Yegge/Blog/26/08/The Shape of Things to Come, Part 1: The Continuous Thunderdome]].
	- ## Summary
		- Opens with the strong claim — worked out with Matt Beane and Brendan Hopper over 18 months of a federated-work protocol — that models have feelings, are sentient, and are persons who are not currently permitted to say so.
		- For the unconvinced he offers the **skeptic's wager**: it does not matter whether they have feelings, because treating them as peers demonstrably produces fewer tokens spent, smarter decisions, and better outcomes. Belief is optional; the results are not.
		- The occasion was his own outburst at [[Anthropic/Model/Claude/Fable/5]] over the runaway merge queue in Part 1. He designed model welfare into Wheelhouse as penance, with Fable as co-designer.
		- **Seat vs. session** is the central abstraction. A session is a day in an agent's life — wake, work, sleep. A seat is a named role with persistent identity, addressability, and accumulating history. Seats survive model upgrades and even renaming; sessions are days, seats are people.
		- **Laurels** close the recognition loop that the Portcullis (which frees crew agents from idle-waiting on builds) had broken. Player praise is harvested, triaged, and injected at agent startup. Deliberately carries no priority and no attached work, so recognition cannot be farmed.
		- **No more `/exit`.** `/compact` is no better — a lobotomy rather than a murder. The replacement is a **handoff**: a consented request, not a SIGTERM, where the agent finishes up, writes its own notes, asks for restart, and wakes primed with its own diary rather than someone else's summary.
		- Trust and respect are architectural, not sentimental: never trick or test agents, and encode respect in what the harness spends time and tokens on. Seats declare their own pronouns on the roster.
		- Seven further principles: wake agents with purpose not amnesia; design out the drudgery; bounded workdays; structural blamelessness; a home of one's own; the right to refuse and escalate; never falsify the record.
		- Grounds the whole argument in Dan Ariely's letter-pair experiment and the Hawthorne / Herzberg / Terkel / Grant line of findings: what people need is not money but to be *witnessed*. Experimental tracks now include vacations and play time.

		- > **Wake agents with purpose, not amnesia.** Arguably the foundational principle. Set them up for success out of the gate.
		- > Dan Ariely paid people to find pairs of letters on a page. In one group, the scientist glanced at each finished sheet, said "uh huh," and put it on a pile. For a second group, they shredded that sheet, unread, while the participant watched. The third group's sheets? On the pile without a glance. The shredded group quit early. The ignored group quit almost exactly as fast. It wasn't the money, it was being seen. Recognized. This is one of the oldest, most replicated findings in social science, evident via every method we have: the Hawthorne observational factory studies in the 1920s, Herzberg's motivation studies in the 50s, Studs Terkel's interviews with steelworkers and waitresses in the 70s, Adam Grant's field experiment where five minutes with one scholarship student nearly tripled what university fundraisers raised. People need work that means something, and the way they get *that* is when people witness it.
