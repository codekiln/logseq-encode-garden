- # Superposition
	- ## Overview
		- In quantum mechanics, superposition is the ability of a quantum system to be in a combination of several states at once. A qubit can be partly 0 and partly 1; an electron can be spread over several positions.
	- ## Key Principles
		- The state is a weighted sum of possible outcomes. The weights, called amplitudes, are complex numbers, and the square of each amplitude's magnitude gives the probability of that outcome when the system is measured.
		- Measurement returns one outcome. After it, the system is found in the state that was measured.
		- Amplitudes can add or cancel, which is how superposed states interfere. The interference pattern in the double-slit experiment comes from this.
	- ## Examples
		- Quantum algorithms put qubits in superposition and then use interference so that the amplitudes of wrong answers cancel and the amplitudes of right answers add up.
		- [[Person/Erwin Schrödinger/Cat]] — [[Person/Erwin Schrödinger]]'s thought experiment carrying an atom's superposition up to a cat that is both dead and alive.
	- ## Misconceptions
		- A superposition is one definite quantum state. It differs from a system that is secretly in one state that we just don't know yet, and interference experiments can tell the two apart.
