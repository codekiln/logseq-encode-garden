- # What is the etymology of "highfalutin"?
	- ## [[AI Answer]]
		- Short answer: no clear consensus, but the leading theories point to **high-flown/high-flying**, not to playing high notes on a flute.
		- First attested as U.S. slang in 1839 (as a noun by 1848), from folk speech rather than any single documented coinage. ([Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/highfalutin%27))
		- Competing theories, none settled:
			- **High-flown / high-flying** — Etymonline's lead guess, and the theory Random House's historical dictionary also favors as most probable. ([Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/highfalutin%27), [Grammarphobia](https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2007/05/a-highfalutin-word.html))
			- **Fluting** — American Heritage Dictionary suggests "high" + "fluting," i.e. someone who thinks too highly of himself is "high-fluting." Merriam-Webster hedges the same way. This is the closest match to the guess above, but even the dictionaries proposing it call it uncertain, and one critic (Grammarphobia) notes it cuts against the etymology since flutes were originally humble farmers' instruments, not symbols of pretension.
			- **Steamboat smokestacks** — a folk theory (via broadcaster Melvyn Bragg) that wealthy 1800s riverboat passengers were called "highfalutin'" because of the "high fluted smokestacks" that carried soot away from them. If "fluting" is the root at all, this architectural/grooved sense (like fluted columns) is the more plausible link, not the musical sense of playing high notes.
			- **Yiddish** — a proposed derivation from "hifelufelem" ("nonsense"), floated as plausible by sound alone but not well evidenced.
		- On the specific guess: "fluting" here, in every version of the theory, means grooved/pipe-shaped (as in a fluted column or a fluted smokestack) or the instrument itself as a symbol, not the act of playing high notes. And it's only one of several competing, unproven theories, not the accepted origin.
