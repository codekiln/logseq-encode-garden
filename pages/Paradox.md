- # Paradox
	- ## Overview
		- A paradox is a statement or situation that seems self-contradictory or absurd yet is supported by reasoning that looks sound. From the Greek *paradoxon*, "contrary to expectation."
	- ## Kinds of paradox
		- **Veridical** — the conclusion sounds absurd but is true. The Monty Hall problem and the birthday paradox, in which 23 people are enough for even odds that two share a birthday, both hold up under calculation.
		- **Falsidical** — the conclusion is false because the argument hides a mistake. Zeno's paradoxes of motion fall apart once an infinite sum of shrinking steps is allowed to add up to a finite distance.
		- **Antinomy** — accepted reasoning leads to a true contradiction, which forces a change to the assumptions. The liar paradox ("this sentence is false") and Russell's paradox about the set of all sets that do not contain themselves are the classic cases; Russell's led to the axioms of modern set theory.
	- ## What paradoxes are good for
		- A paradox marks the spot where an intuition or a theory breaks. Working one out usually means giving up an assumption nobody had noticed they were making, which is why paradoxes have driven changes in logic, mathematics and physics.
		- [[Person/Erwin Schrödinger/Cat]] is a paradox built on purpose, to show where a theory of quantum measurement leads.
	- ## Examples in the garden
		- [[Jevon's Paradox]] — efficiency gains that make a resource cheaper can raise its total use.
