- # [[Inflation]] vs [[Deflation]]
	- ## Summary
		- Inflation is a rising general price level; deflation is a falling general price level. The trade-off is not a choice between two equally desirable targets: rapid or unpredictable movement in either direction can disrupt decisions, while persistent deflation can worsen downturns and make debts harder to repay.
		- Modern central banks generally aim for low, stable inflation rather than a perfectly constant price level. That leaves room for relative prices to adjust while reducing the risks associated with high inflation and deflation.

	- ## How to read local examples
		- Lower prices for a particular service are not deflation unless the overall price level is falling. Higher home values are asset-price inflation in ordinary speech, but they are not the same measure as consumer-price inflation.
		- [Federal Reserve: Historical approaches to monetary policy](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/historical-approaches-to-monetary-policy.htm) describes the costs of unstable inflation and deflation, including harder debt repayment when prices fall unexpectedly.
		- [St. Louis Fed: Inflation, disinflation, and deflation](https://www.stlouisfed.org/open-vault/2023/august/explaining-inflation-disinflation-deflation) distinguishes inflation from disinflation and deflation.
