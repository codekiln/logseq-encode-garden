- # Music as a Gradual Process
	- ## Summary
		- A short set of observations and aphoristic statements by [[Person/Steve Reich]], written in New Mexico in the summer of 1968 and first published in the catalogue to the exhibition *Anti-Illusion: Procedures/Materials* at the Whitney Museum of American Art in 1969.
		- Reich writes about pieces of music that are themselves processes, which should be audible and should happen extremely gradually. He contrasts them with [[Person/John Cage]]'s chance procedures and with serial music, where the process can't be heard in the sounding music.
		- Reprinted in Reich's *Writings on Music, 1965–2000* (Oxford University Press, 2002), pp. 34–36. The whole book is also in Reader: [Writings on Music, 1965–2000](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m701et41bm9ja7hn39zmawvb).
	- ## Notes
		- What "process" means in the essay
			- > I do not mean the process of composition but rather pieces of music that are, literally, processes.
			  id:: 84beb931-ac2e-4ec3-9233-a6d7da96814d
			- > What I’m interested in is a compositional process and a sounding music that are one and the same thing.
		- Control and acceptance
			- > By “a kind” of complete control, I mean that by running this material through this process I completely control all that results, but also that I accept all that results without changes.
		- Improvisation
			- > Nevertheless, these modal musics remain more or less strict frameworks for improvisation. They are not processes.
			- > The distinctive thing about musical processes is that they determine all the note-to-note details and the overall form simultaneously. One can’t improvise in a musical process—the concepts are mutually exclusive.
			  id:: 81155a77-837e-4f46-aaac-25d53beb1cbb
			- The phrase "no room for improvisation" is Christensen's paraphrase of Reich (2003), quoted in Erik Schoster's paper [Where do you place agency in a process-based music system](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hyaqn8fjp2jjfm2x2gv2znc9). Reich's own sentence is the one above.
		- What remains outside the composer's intentions
			- > These mysteries are the impersonal, unintended, psychoacoustic by-products of the intended process. These might include submelodies heard within repeated melodic patterns, stereophonic effects due to listener location, slight irregularities in performance, harmonics, difference tones, and so on.
	- ## Highlights
		- > It is quite natural to think about musical processes if one is frequently working with electromechanical sound equipment. All music turns out to be ethnic music.
			- [Readwise](https://readwise.io/open/1072204285)
		- > While performing and listening to gradual musical processes, one can participate in a particular liberating and impersonal kind of ritual. Focusing in on the musical process makes possible that shift of attention away from he and she and you and me outward toward it.
