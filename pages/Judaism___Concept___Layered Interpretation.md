- # Layered Interpretation in Rabbinic Judaism
	- ## Overview
		- [[Judaism/Rabbinic]] is organized around a layered tradition in which later texts interpret earlier texts, preserve the arguments about those interpretations, and then become objects of interpretation themselves. The stack runs from the [[Judaism/Torah]] through the [[Judaism/Mishnah]], the [[Judaism/Gemara]] and the [[Judaism/Talmud]] to medieval commentary and commentary on commentary, with no terminal layer.
		- The distinctive feature is that the history of interpretation becomes part of the thing being interpreted. The tradition preserves a structured record of the interpretive community arguing about the answer, and that record acquires authority of its own.
	- ## The stack
		- ### [[Judaism/Torah]]
			- The five books of Moses command without specifying. "Remember the Sabbath day and keep it holy" says nothing about what counts as work, when the day begins, or what may be carried. Interpretation is unavoidable from the first layer.
		- ### [[Judaism/Mishnah]]
			- Compiled around 200 CE under [[Person/Judah ha-Nasi]], the Mishnah reorganizes the law by subject and records disagreement: Rabbi A says X, Rabbi B says Y. It preserves the argument along with the ruling.
		- ### [[Judaism/Gemara]]
			- Centuries of rabbinic discussion of the Mishnah. The Gemara asks why a rabbi holds his view, which verse supports him, whether another Mishnah contradicts him, who the anonymous speaker is, and what unstated assumption would reconcile two statements. Dead ends, objections, hypothetical cases, minority positions and unresolved questions all stay in the record.
		- ### [[Judaism/Talmud]]
			- Mishnah plus Gemara. The Babylonian Talmud, in its received form from the fifth and sixth centuries, became the more influential of the two; the Jerusalem Talmud is a century or so older.
		- ### Commentary on commentary
			- [[Person/Rashi]], the Tosafists, [[Person/Maimonides]] and later authorities interpret the Talmudic discussion; responsa and later commentary interpret how those interpretations relate. Each layer can become the subject of the next.
	- ## Written Torah and Oral Torah
		- Rabbinic Judaism understands revelation as both the [[Judaism/Torah/Written]] and the [[Judaism/Torah/Oral]], the interpretive tradition needed to understand and practice it. Interpretation is built into the transmission of revelation, so the chain of interpretation carries religious significance, and disagreement within it counts as part of the tradition rather than as a failure to recover it.
	- ## The printed page
		- A traditional page of Talmud puts the Mishnah and Gemara at the center, with Rashi's commentary, the Tosafists' discussions, cross-references and later notes arranged around them. The page looks like generations of people talking around a text, a spatial representation of a historical conversation. A modern printed Bible, by contrast, shows the biblical text with notes beneath it.
	- ## Contradiction as a source of reasoning
		- Later rabbis read an earlier authoritative text for more than what its author historically meant. An apparent contradiction between two authoritative statements becomes a prompt: what distinction must exist for both to make sense? Textual friction becomes intellectual productivity.
	- ## Meta-interpretive, not meta-narrative
		- The Talmud contains narratives about rabbis, biblical figures, God, dreams and miracles, but the structure that matters here is meta-interpretive, or recursive textuality: a sacred textual record of people working out how sacred texts should be read.
